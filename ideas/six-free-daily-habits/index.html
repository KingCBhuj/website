Six Free Daily Habits to Reclaim Your Health

No gym, no supplements, no extra rupee. Just small changes to how you eat, sit, move and rest.

By Chirag Chande · 17 November 2026

Getting healthier does not have to begin with buying something. Before signing up for another programme or searching for another product, look at what you already do each day: how you eat, how you sit, how you move and when you wind down. Traditional Indian life holds several simple habits worth revisiting. They cost nothing, take a few minutes, and fit into the day you already have. I share them as habits I value, and I have tried to be clear about what is tradition, what is personal experience and what research supports, so you can decide what suits you.

Why digestion comes first: Jatharagni

Ayurveda places great importance on Jatharagni, commonly translated as “digestive fire.” In this traditional framework it describes the body’s capacity to digest food and turn it into nourishment, so the focus is not only on what we eat but on how well we digest it.

Jatharagni is a traditional concept, not a literal flame in the stomach, and it is not a direct synonym for stomach acid or any single modern biological process. Think of it as a useful lens: several habits below are simply ways of making digestion easier, such as eating warm fresh food, pausing after meals and not eating too late.

1. Sit upright for a few minutes after meals

Instead of dropping onto the sofa the moment you finish eating, take a short, quiet pause. Vajrasana is the traditional way to do it: kneel with your hips resting towards your heels, your back upright and your hands on your thighs. Five to ten minutes is plenty.

If kneeling is uncomfortable, sit upright on a chair or place a folded towel between your calves and thighs. Comfort matters more than the exact pose.

The better-supported advice is about not lying down too soon after eating. For people with night-time acid reflux, the NIDDK suggests finishing meals at least three hours before lying down. That does not prove Vajrasana itself has a special digestive benefit, but staying upright after a meal is a simple, free way to follow the same spirit.

2. Make room for squats

The deep squat was once an everyday resting position in India and across the world, used for cooking, eating and working. Chairs and commodes have made it unfamiliar, but you can bring a little of it back.

Try five slow squats while the kettle boils or between tasks at your desk. Hold a door frame or wall for support if you need it, and go only as deep as you can control. Regular squatting keeps the hips, knees and ankles moving and works the legs and core, and your range tends to improve over a few weeks.

You do not need a perfect deep squat on day one. A shallow, steady one counts.

3. Prefer sitting on the floor to hanging legs

Sukhasana, the simple cross-legged seat, is how many of us sat as children. Sitting this way while reading, talking or eating asks the hips and lower back to work gently, whereas hours on a sofa or chair with legs hanging tend to leave the body stiff. Staying in one position for a very long time is rarely good for us, so varying how you sit is worth the effort.

Cross your legs the other way every so often, and use a cushion if it helps. You do not have to give up your chair. Just make the floor one of your regular seats, especially at mealtimes and in the evening, and never hold a position that hurts.

4. Make warm, home-cooked meals your default

When you cook at home, you decide the ingredients, oil, salt and portions. Outside food is usually made to please the tongue and the seller’s budget, not your long-term health, and it often means reused oil, heavy masalas, refined flour and hidden sugar.

A simple meal of dal, sabzi and roti, eaten warm and freshly made, is easy on the stomach and easy on the wallet. It costs a fraction of a restaurant meal and needs nothing fashionable or labelled a “superfood.”

This is not a ban on eating out. Enjoy it when you wish, but let home food be the usual choice and outside food the occasional treat. If time or access to a kitchen is limited, start with one more home-cooked meal a week.

5. Sip warm water through the day

I find warm water soothing and I value it as a daily habit, so I keep a flask nearby and sip through the day. It also nudges me away from sugary drinks.

Drink it comfortably warm, never scalding. If you prefer room-temperature or cool water, there is no need to change: research has not shown that warm water is generally better for health. What matters more is staying hydrated. Water needs differ from person to person, so let thirst guide you, and drink more in hot weather, during exercise or when unwell. Older adults can have a weaker thirst response, and anyone with a medical condition should follow their doctor’s advice on fluids.

6. Keep dinner light and early

Traditional advice is to eat dinner while there is still daylight, so the body can digest before it rests. The practical measure today is the gap between dinner and bedtime. Leaving at least three hours before you lie down may help, especially if you get night-time reflux.

Keep the meal simple. Khichdi, or roti with sabzi, is light without being inadequate, and a bowl of soup alone may not be enough for everyone. If you eat late some days, do not worry; aim for a routine you can keep rather than a deadline to feel guilty about.

Begin with one habit

You do not need to adopt all six tomorrow. Pick the one that fits your life most easily: sip warm water, cook one more meal at home, do a few squats, sit on the floor this evening, or finish dinner a little earlier. Notice what feels good, and add another when the first feels natural.

Tradition offers ideas, research helps test specific claims, and your own experience tells you what works for you. Small habits, repeated daily, are where lasting change tends to begin, and none of them costs a rupee.

These are general wellness suggestions, not treatments or a substitute for medical care. Do not force a posture or continue through pain. If you have joint problems, a recent injury or surgery, are pregnant, or have a condition affecting food or fluid intake, seek individual medical guidance.
