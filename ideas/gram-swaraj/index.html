Decentralisation, consent, and local spending

An idea I’m exploring—not a finished argument.

By Chirag Chande · 27 October 2026

Could part of the answer lie in decentralisation, in the spirit of Gram Swaraj?

What if major local public spending required broad consent from the people of the area concerned—and those communities had a direct role in providing the resources?

The principle I want to explore is this: no unilateral taxation for major projects. A proposal would need to earn the community’s support. Without broad consent, the project would wait, be revised, or make way for a different priority.

I’m drawn to the connection between decision-making, contribution, and accountability. But I’m also trying to understand where this principle would work—and where it might fall short.

Questions I’m thinking about

  • What should “broad consent” mean: a majority vote, a higher threshold, or something else?
  • Should resources come from voluntary contributions, locally approved taxes, or a combination?
  • How would a community fund essential services for people who cannot afford to contribute?
  • How would poorer areas obtain the resources they need?
  • What happens when a project benefits several communities, or when urgent action cannot wait?
  • How do we protect minority interests without allowing a small group to block every decision?

Could this approach make public spending more accountable, or would it make collective action too difficult? What would make it fair and workable?

I’d welcome thoughtful objections, practical examples, and alternative approaches—especially arguments that challenge my assumptions.
